Real-time measurement of temperature
distribution inside a gain medium of
a diode-pumped Er
3+/Yb3+ 1.55 μm laser

A scheme to measure temperature distribution inside a gain
medium of a diode-pumped solid-state laser is proposed
based on the fluorescence intensity ratio technique. By re-
cording the temperature-dependent upconversion fluores-
cence related to the
2H11∕2 → 4I15∕2 and 4S3∕2 → 4I15∕2
transitions of Er3/.0135, the real-time temperature distribution
inside an Er3/.0135:Yb3/.0135:LuAl3/.0133BO3/.01344 crystal end-pumped by
a 976 nm diode laser can be measured while a 1.55 μm laser
is operating. Influences of pump power, output power, and
output mirror transmissivity on the temperature distribu-
tion inside the gain medium are investigated. This scheme
can conveniently and accurately measure the temperature
distribution along the laser path in a rare-earth-doped gain
medium in real time.

Due to the quantum defects caused by energy differences
between pump and laser photons, as well as multiphonon non-
radiative transitions between some related multiplets of doped
active ions, pump-induced heat is inevitably generated in gain
media of solid-state lasers [ 1,2]. The pump-induced thermal
effects limit the output power, operation efficiency, and beam
quality and even cause the fracture of gain media [ 1,3].
Temperature distribution can directly reflect the thermal effects
in gain media. Therefore, when a laser is operating, the accurate
and real-time measurement of temperature distribution inside
the gain medium is meaningful. It is conducive to designing
laser devices for reducing the thermal effects and then enhanc-
ing the laser performance.
Surface temperatures of gain media can be measured directly
by infrared cameras [ 3,4]. However, this method cannot mea-
sure the internal temperatures of gain media and the measured
values are easily disturbed by the infrared signals reflected from
other surfaces. Temperature distributions inside gain media can
be simulated theoretically based on the finite-element-analysis
method [5,6]. However, because some hypotheses are adopted
in the establishment of the theoretical model and some uncer-
tain thermo-optic coefficients are used in the simulating proc-
ess, the result may have a deviation from the actual situation.
Utilizing the temperature-dependent feature of fluorescence
spectra originating from two thermally coupled levels (TCLs) of
some rare earth (RE) ions, the fluorescence intensity ratio (FIR)
technique can realize a temperature measurement at the lumi-
nous point, where FIR represents the ratio of the integrated
intensities I of the two fluorescence bands related to the
TCLs. This technique can be used in harsh environments, such
as high-voltage power stations and oil refineries [ 7,8], with its
unique advantages of noncontact, high accuracy and sensitivity,
and excellent anti-interference. For a diode-pumped solid-state
laser with high pump power density, upconversion (UC) visible
fluorescence is often observed in a gain medium doped with RE
ions, such as Nd
3/.0135, Yb3/.0135, Er3/.0135, Tm3/.0135, and Ho3/.0135[9–11]. UC
fluorescence is easily detected because it can be seen by naked
eyes and is generally away from the pump and laser wave-
lengths. Then, by measuring the temperature-dependent UC
fluorescence intensities originating from two TCLs of RE ions
along the laser path in the gain medium, the temperature
distribution along the path can be obtained when a laser is
operating.
The
2H11∕2 and 4S3∕2 levels of Er3/.0135with energy separations
of about 780 cm −1 are well-known TCLs [7,8]. UC green fluo-
rescence bands originating from the 2H11∕2 → 4I15∕2 and
4S3∕2 → 4I15∕2 transitions of Er3/.0135can be observed under ex-
citation at 980 nm for an Er3/.0135∕Yb3/.0135-codoped material via the
energy transfer from Yb3/.0135to Er3/.0135[8,10]. Presently, temper-
ature measurements of Er3/.0135∕Yb3/.0135-codoped optical fibers and
phosphors as optical temperature sensors and heat-sensitive
materials have been widely investigated based on the FIR tech-
nique [ 8,12]. In this work, we report on the first successful
measurement of real-time temperature distribution along the

A c-cut, 0.7 mm thick Er(1.1 at. %):Yb(24.1 at. %):LuAB
crystal with cross section of 5m m × 5m m was polished and
used in the spectral and laser experiments. To obtain a relation-
ship between the intensity ratio of UC fluorescence bands origi-
nating from the TCLs and the crystal temperature, the crystal
was placed in a heating stove with a temperature control range
from 300 to 540 K and an accuracy of /.00060.1 K . Unpolarized
UC fluorescence of the whole crystal was collected and detected
from the surface with a cross section of 0.7 mm × 5m m when
a 980 nm laser diode (LD) (MDL-III-980, CNI) was used as
the exciting source. In order to ensure a low thermal loading
generated by the LD excitation and the uniformity of temper-
ature in the whole sample, the pump power was limited to
0.5 W and the spot diameter of the pump beam was about
5 mm. Then, the UC fluorescence spectra from 510 to
570 nm of the Er:Yb:LuAB crystal were recorded by a spec-
trometer (FLS920, Edinburgh) in a temperature interval of
30 K and are shown in Fig. 1(a). There are two discrete bands
around 520 and 540 nm, corresponding to the transitions of
2H11∕2 → 4I15∕2 and 4S3∕2 → 4I15∕2, respectively. For the sake
of brevity, the transitions of 2H11∕2 → 4I15∕2 and 4S3∕2 →
4I15∕2 are represented by 520 and 540 hereinafter, respectively.

The laser experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2. A continu-
ous-wave (cw) 976 nm fiber-coupled diode laser with a 100 μm
diameter core was used as the pumping source, which excites
the Yb
3/.0135and Er3/.0135ions to the same levels as the 980 nm LD
used above. The pumping beam was focused to a spot with a
waist diameter of 100 μm in the Er:Yb:LuAB crystal by using a
telescopic lens system (TLS). The uncoated Er:Yb:LuAB crystal
and a 1.2 mm thick sapphire crystal were placed in a copper
holder and closely contacted by two copper pieces with screws
at front and rear faces. The copper holder was cooled by water
at 293 K. The sapphire crystal was used as a heat sink to reduce
the thermal effects of the gain medium and then improve the
output laser performances [ 2,15]. An input mirror (IM) with
90% transmissivity at 976 nm and 99.8% reflectivity at
1.55 μm was directly deposited onto the front surface of the
sapphire crystal. Two output mirrors (OMs) with identical cur-
vature radii of 100 mm but different transmissivities of 1.7%
and 5.0% at 1.55 μm were used. The cavity length was close
to 100 mm. By using a convex lens with a focal length of
27 mm, an image of the UC fluorescence signal emitting from
a 0.7 mm × 5m m surface of the Er:Yb:LuAB crystal was
obtained with a magnification of 1.4. A high-resolution optical
fiber spectrometer with a fiber diameter of 100 μm (QE65000,
Ocean Optics) was used to scan the image and record the
fluorescence signal point by point when a 1.55 μm laser was
operating. Scattering signals originating from the pump and
fundamental lasers were obstructed by a filter.

Figure 3 shows the cw output power realized in the
Er:Yb:LuAB crystal versus incident pump power for different
OM transmissivities. For the 5.0% transmissivity, a laser with a
maximum output power of 0.86 W and a slope efficiency of
22% was obtained at an incident pump power of 5.11 W.
The incident pump threshold was 1.17 W. For the 1.7% trans-
missivity, a laser with a maximum output power of 0.76 W and
a slope efficiency of 19% was obtained at an incident pump
power of 5.11 W. The incident pump threshold was
0.87 W. The beam quality factors M
2x and M 2y were measured
to be about 5.71 and 5.33 at a pump power of 5.11 W, respec-
tively, and they changed with the pump power.
When the pump power was 5.11 W and OM transmissivity
was 5.0%, UC fluorescence signals emitting from different
positions along the laser path with an interval of 0.07 mm were
detected through moving the probe of the optical fiber
spectrometer along the crystal image after the convex lens.
The result is shown in Fig. 4(a). Based on Eq. ( 2), the corre-
sponding temperature at each position can be calculated with a
maximum error of /.000612 K and is shown in Fig. 4(b).A ta
pump power of 5.11 W, the highest temperature in the crystal
is 710 K at the position of 0.35 mm from the input surface.
The temperature at the input surface of the Er:Yb:LuAB crystal
is 450 K and about 100 K lower than that at the output surface.
Under identical experimental conditions, temperature distribu-
tions for pump powers of 3.74 and 2.63 W were also measured
and are also shown in Fig. 4(b). Compared with the result
obtained at a pump power of 5.11 W, the trend of temperature
distribution inside the crystal is similar but the highest temper-
atures decrease 150 and 220 K, respectively.
The temperature distribution measured in this work is
similar to that inside the Er:Yb:YAB crystal calculated by
the finite-element-analysis method [ 15], in which the highest
temperature in the crystal with sapphire cooling was 800 K
when the pump power was 5 W. According to [ 15], the tem-
perature distribution in the crystal without sapphire cooling is
similar to the distribution of the pump power density [see
Fig. 4(c)]. For diode-pumped Nd:YAG and Yb:YAG lasers,
the reported highest temperatures in these crystals were
393 and 335 K with pump power densities of 191 and
318 W ∕mm
2 [4,6], respectively. They are much lower than
that (491 K) measured in this work when the pump power den-
sity is 335 W ∕mm
2, which corresponds to an incident pump
power of 2.63 W. Compared with the Nd3/.0135and Yb3/.0135lasers,
more absorbed pump power is converted to heat for the
Er:Yb:LuAB laser because it has a larger quantum defect
(37% with respect to 24% of an 807 nm diode-pumped
Nd
3/.01351.06 μm laser and 6% of a 976 nm diode pumped
Yb3/.01351.04 μm laser, respectively) and a lower fluorescence
quantum efficiency of the upper laser level 4I13∕2 (7% with
respect to about 90% of both Nd3/.0135:YAG and Yb3/.0135:YAG
crystals).
Cyclic measurements of the temperature at the position of
0.28 mm from the input face of the Er:Yb:LuAB crystal, where
the strongest UC fluorescence signal was observed, were per-
formed by changing the pump power. The result indicates that
the temperature measurement in this work has a good repeat-
ability, as shown in Fig. 4(d).
At a certain pump power, the output power of the laser can
be gradually changed by adjusting the cavity alignment.

λf is the average fluorescence wavelength, and λl is the laser
wavelength. Because ηr of the Er:Yb:LuAB crystal is lower than
10% [ 2], ηh in the crystal increases with the decrement of ηl ,
which is proportional to the reduction of the output power at a
fixed pump power. Therefore, with the reduction of the output
power, the temperature inside the crystal increases, as shown in
Fig. 5. It also means that an efficient laser operation would be
beneficial in reducing the fractional thermal load in the crystal.
The temperature distribution at a pump power of 5.11 W
and the temperature at the position of 0.28 mm for different
pump powers were also measured at OM transmissivity of
1.7%. The results obtained at OM transmissivities of 1.7%
and 5.0% are compared in Figs. 6(a) and 6(b), which exhibit
similar variation trends with maximum measurement errors of
/.000612 K . With the increment of the pump threshold, the heat
generated in the crystal is increased. Therefore, the highest tem-
perature inside the crystal at an OM transmissivity of 5.0% is
about 40 K higher than that at an OM transmissivity of 1.7%,
as shown in Fig. 6(a), while the pump thresholds are 1.17 and
0.87 W for the former and later, respectively.
In summary, a measurement of temperature distribution
along laser path inside an Er:Yb:LuAB crystal has been success-
fully realized when a 1.55 μm laser is operating. The highest
temperature inside the crystal is 710 K when the pump power is
5.11 W and the output power is 0.86 W. Compared with the
existing thermometries, this method can realize the real-time
temperature measurement inside a gain medium with advan-
tages of high accuracy and excellent anti-interference. This
method may be used to measure the temperature distributions
along any direction inside RE-doped gain media with UC fluo-
rescence emitting from TCLs during laser operation.
